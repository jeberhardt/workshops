How a breadboard connects

Rails run the full length of each side. Every hole in a rail is joined. Use red (+) for power and blue (−) for ground.

Rows join five holes side by side: A to E is one strip, F to J is another.

The gap splits every row in two, so row 3 on the left isn’t joined to row 3 on the right.

Two legs in the same strip are connected. That’s why each part’s legs go in different rows.

Show a real breadboard while this is up, or peel back the sticky backing on a spare one to show the metal clips underneath. The rails are for sharing power and ground: one wire from the Pi's 3.3V to the red rail and one from GND to the blue rail, and every part can tap in nearby. The rows are where parts connect to each other: put two legs in the same strip of five and they're wired together. The centre gap is sized for chips and buttons to straddle, so each side gets its own strip. This is also how the Fritzing diagrams read: a part counts as connected to whatever else shares its row. Watch for two common mistakes: a part with both legs in the same row (it's shorted, so it does nothing), and longer breadboards whose rails are split halfway down, shown by a break in the red and blue lines. Bridge the halves with a short jumper.
